How deep can an exact drafter go? A 30-token draft head for a 125B mixture-of-experts model on a 64 GB Mac

Kamen Levi

Independent researcher

23 November 2026

PDF

Abstract

Speculative decoding makes a large model faster by letting a small drafter guess several tokens that the large model then checks in one pass. In exact mode the output must stay bit-identical to one-token greedy decoding, so the drafter can change only the speed, never the text. We ask how far ahead an exact drafter can usefully guess for Qwen3.8-Flash-Next, a 125B mixture-of-experts model (48 layers, 512 routed experts per layer, 10 per token, hybrid gated-delta-net and attention layers) whose experts stream from the internal SSD of a MacBook Pro (M5 Pro, 64 GB).

We train the model's own multi-token-prediction (MTP) layer to chain 30 tokens deep with a 30-step training-time test. We call this head t30b. We measure it on 24 held-out HumanEval+ problems and 100 held-out MBPP problems. Five findings:

  1. The deep loss is drift, not missing knowledge. Drafting from its own carried state, the head's per-guess accuracy on HumanEval+ falls from 97.95 % at the first guess to 89.65 % at guesses 23-30 (MBPP: 94.01 % to 72.13 %) [K]. Given the 125B's true state at each drafted position, every guess is as good as a first guess: 97.7-98.0 % at every depth on HumanEval+ and 93.6-94.0 % on MBPP [LAB]. About 5x more training data added 2.3 points at guesses 23-30, and twice the trainable capacity added nothing [K].
  2. Accuracy must be measured where checks start. A check starts right after the previous check's first miss, and misses cluster. There the first guess is right only 91.97 % of the time on HumanEval+ and 89.70 % on MBPP [K]; about half of this dip comes from the 125B's own close calls. The first 15 guesses decide the total: raising them to 98 % gives 22.63 of the 23.81 tokens a flat 98 % drafter keeps per 32-row check, while raising guesses 15-30 alone adds 0.6 [P].
  3. The right token is usually close, and trees use that. At check starts the 125B's token is in the head's top 4 in 98.5 % of cases on HumanEval+ (99.0 % on MBPP), and the head's own confidence separates easy starts from hard ones [K]. A 16-node EAGLE-2-style tree keeps 6.0 % more tokens per check than a 16-token chain on HumanEval+ and 13.6 % more on MBPP [K]. No tree up to 128 nodes holds 98 % per depth band to depth 30 [P].
  4. The 125B decides late. A lens on its partial state locates the head's misses well only in its last 4 of 48 layers [K], so no cheap partial-depth readout can stand in for the true state. No drift fix we screened closed more than a small part of the gap; the best, a second small model (2B or 4B) that audits the head's drafts, raised kept tokens by 5-12 % [K].
  5. Exact tree checks are harder on a hybrid model. A 16-node tree flipped one near-tie at token 95 of one MBPP answer. A 16-token chain under a fixed, row-count-invariant kernel set (the "reference contract") matched its own one-token decoding on 16 of 16 prompts: 2,604 of 2,604 kept-row logit hashes [K].

An exact 32-row check on this Mac cannot cost less than about 58 ms on code (its byte floor [P]; it measures 90-102 ms today [K]). At that floor, with free drafting, a drafter that held 98 % to depth 30 would cap near 410 tok/s on code and a perfect one near 550 [P]. With t30b the exact 16-token chain is priced at about 130-160 tok/s on code; it was not timed at the 45 GB setting [P].
