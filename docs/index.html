What It Takes to Run a 125B LLM Fast on a 64 GB Mac, and How to Go Faster

Kamen Levi

9 November 2026

We run a 125B-parameter mixture-of-experts model (Qwen3.8-Flash-Next: 48 layers, 512 routed experts per layer, 10 per token) on one MacBook Pro (M5 Pro, 18-core CPU, 20-core GPU, 64 GB, 2 TB SSD), with the routed experts streamed from the internal SSD at about 3.2 bits per weight and the process's lifetime peak at most 45 GB on the benchmark prompts (plus a 7.45 GB expert tier that macOS may reclaim; longer prompts go over it, section 6.1). The pieces are mostly published; this paper is about how they depend on each other and what each adds. Speculative decoding pays one pass of the large model for up to 16 drafted tokens, but it is exact only if that pass computes the same bits as one-token decoding. Batch-invariant kernels on the GPU and its matrix units make it so, and we audit it on every run (96 of 96 code runs, 24 of 24 chat and 24 of 24 math runs token-identical to one-token greedy decoding). Exactness then turns every later optimisation into a test of token equality that takes minutes: the levers of the streamed engine, such as per-layer expert pools and expert reads hidden behind the GPU's work, were each adopted that way. On top of the exact engine, a lossy layer lets drafted rows of code answers skip cold experts and run 3-bit parts, and ships only if it scores the same as the exact reference on the model's benchmarks.

The exact configuration decodes 101.4 tok/s on 12 HumanEval prompts, 70.4 on 12 MBPP, 77.9 on 12 math and 37.1 on 12 chat (IFEval) prompts, at a lifetime peak of 41.7-42.5 GB [K]. The lossy configuration we ship scores the same as the exact reference on the model card's IFBench, GPQA Diamond and LiveCodeBench v6 in both prompt orders, and decodes 164.0, 112.7, 78.9 and 45.2 tok/s on the same sets at 42.4-43.2 GB [K]. Our first token is slow, a median of 2.7-4.6 s on these short prompts: on the same machine and prompts the open engine Strata's takes 0.54-1.01 s, and Strata wins or ties every whole request of up to 256 tokens, although our lossy decode is 2.5-3.5x its decode on code [K]. None of the methods is specific to this model's layers; we tested one model on one machine. Section 12 describes each finding that, to our knowledge, has not been published before, with its evidence, what it gives and its limits, including one idea that did not pay off. We also report negative results, including three headline numbers we corrected or withdrew.

Batch-invariant kernels on Apple's M5 GPU and its matrix units

Kamen Levi

9 November 2026

Speculative decoding is exact only if verifying k drafted tokens in one batched call produces the same bits as decoding them one at a time. On Apple's M5 GPU with MLX 0.32.2 it does not. The framework picks a different kernel for 1, 2-8, 16 and thousands of rows (vector kernels, split-K kernels, matrix-unit GEMMs), and each rounds differently. With the stock kernels, all 16 rows of a verify pass already differ from one-token decoding at the first matmul of a 125B mixture-of-experts model [K].

We build a batch-invariant kernel set for that model (48 layers, 512 experts per layer, 10 per token; the expert store runs at about 3.2 bits per expert weight):

  • dense projections padded to one 16-row shape, on a matrix-unit kernel that multiplies the raw codes;
  • routed experts on one 16-row matrix-unit tile per expert, then a fixed split-K over a record layout native to the matrix unit's fragments;
  • a hyper-connection read that replicates MLX's own split-K loop bit for bit;
  • attention pinned to fixed key buckets and length classes.

Under it, 16 one-token steps and one 16-row verify agree on all 37 records of a forward through a 12-layer slice [K], and full-length audits agree on every kept row ([MAIN] section 3.2). The dense part of a check falls from 56.8 to 27.4 ms [K]. On the expert path the matrix-unit kernel beats our ALU kernel by x1.062 in whole decode in an engine A/B [K]. Batching a prompt's MoE layers over all chunks is exact on 1.6K-7.9K-token prompts once two kernels are pinned, and prefills them x1.14-1.21 faster [K]. A short last chunk sharing the batched call still breaks it, as in a 30K-token prompt; computing the router and the shared expert per chunk fixes that in a research copy [K]. We map which MLX kernels are row-invariant at decode and prefill widths, and report ten MLX findings with reproductions, among them a sorted-gather bug that leaves rows unwritten past 32,767 rows (independently of its upstream report) and stale reads of CPU-written memory inside one command buffer.

Where we lose. One-token decoding through the padded kernels runs at x0.75 of the stock kernels [K]. Our exact expert kernel runs at 70-74 % of the rate its own loads stream at [K]. The batched prefill peaks at 46.2 GB, over the 45 GB line [K], and it is not exact when a prompt's last chunk is short, as at 30K tokens (section 4.6) [K]. And on the same machine and the same 48 prompts, against Strata (MIT; github.com/Niko1221/Strata), our decode is ahead on code and math and about level on chat, exact chat decode is behind, and Strata's first token (0.5-1.0 s) beats ours (about 2.7-4.6 s), so up to 256 generated tokens Strata wins or ties every whole-request number ([MAIN] Appendix G has the table).

Certifying lossy decoding on the model's known benchmarks: same-scores gates for a 125B mixture-of-experts model on one 64 GB Mac

Kamen Levi

9 November 2026

Lossy decoding is what makes a 125B mixture-of-experts model fast on one 64 GB Mac ([MAIN]), and it changes the output. We ask a narrow question: does a lossy configuration score the same as its exact reference on the model's known benchmarks?

  • The gate: pre-registered and paired, one benchmark at a time.
    • The paired accuracy delta must be above −5 points, and the exact two-sided McNemar test must not reject (p >= 0.05).
    • It runs in two prompt orders, against the exact one-token greedy reference of the same memory mode.
  • Exactness is audited separately: every token, and the logits row behind every kept token, must be bitwise equal to the reference's own one-token decode.
  • What passed, 20 October to 9 November 2026 [K]:
    • L + COH, the 45 GB configuration we publish, and the chat configuration (M1-B + COH) passed the model card's IFBench, GPQA Diamond and LiveCodeBench v6 in both orders.
    • Its certified base ("v1' + wired"; L adds to it a bundle of kernel changes that is bitwise in the exact mode) passed all 10 of its pre-registered lines: HumanEval+, MBPP, math and IFEval in both orders, a pooled line and a long-code line. A certificate covers only the configuration it tested, so it does not carry to L + COH.
    • Exact decode paths that differ only in numerics (REF2, REF2b, NAX6) passed the same gate against each other.
    • Four more lossy configurations passed on the benchmarks they were gated on: 8-bit mixers; a short stack gate; a 32 GB point on HumanEval+ only; the chat configuration on IFEval (before its card gate).
  • What failed [K]:
    • six quality-defined modes;
    • a code profile on the −5-point boundary;
    • every 12 GB candidate we gated on task scores.
  • Where we lose:
    • The gate is coarse. Exact paths that differ only in numerics moved one benchmark by 8 points on 50 items [K]. An arm with no true loss fails one order of one benchmark with a probability we price at 5-15 % [P].
    • Most of our gates ran on subsets of public benchmarks and on home-made lines. The model card's own benchmarks (IFBench, GPQA Diamond, LiveCodeBench v6) were adopted as the gate on 8 November, on 381 keys: IFBench 150 of 300, GPQA Diamond 100 of 198 and one 131-problem LiveCodeBench window. L + COH has passed them, and so has the chat configuration (M1-B + COH; 6 of 6 lines); the 32 GB point (zs32) is not gated (no card gate ran).
    • We list our mistakes, among them a reference switched after we had seen a result, and a chat path we called exact without having measured it.

Drafting for a 125B mixture-of-experts model that does not fit in RAM: training its own MTP head, one drafter per text type, and the levers that failed

Kamen Levi

9 November 2026

We study the drafter side of exact speculative decoding for Qwen3.8-Flash-Next, a 125B mixture-of-experts model (48 layers, 512 routed experts per layer, 10 per token) whose experts stream from the internal SSD of one MacBook Pro (M5 Pro, 18-core CPU, 20-core GPU, 64 GB, 2 TB SSD) ([MAIN]). The engine verifies up to 16 drafted rows per check and keeps the output bit-identical to one-token greedy decoding, so a drafter can change speed but never the output.

We compare two drafters: a 5-layer block drafter that proposes 16 positions in one pass (DFlash2-style), and the model's own multi-token-prediction (MTP) layer, run as a chain, as MTPLX does. Three findings:

  • Training the MTP layer the way it is used, over its own chain (EAGLE-3's training-time test), raises its code kept tokens per 16-row check from 7.78 to 10.98 (x1.41) [K]. That is above the block drafter's 10.44 on the same answers. Chat moves by only 2-3 %, not significant.
  • The chained MTP head beats the block drafter on chat and agent text (x1.08-1.32 tokens per check [K]) and loses on code (x0.75). So the engine routes by text type. On 19 fresh chat prompts the switch gave:
    • whole decode 23.1 -> 28.8 tok/s (+24.5 %);
    • whole request 18.6 -> 22.0 tok/s (+18.3 %);
    • first token 1.9 s in both arms;
    • lifetime peak 37.9 GB;
    • every token identical.
  • Most other levers failed. Of 83 drafter-side items we tried (Appendix A), 41 ended in a KILL (by a pre-registered line, or at the desk or on paper), and 27 ended between lines, parked or as information.

Where we lose is chat. We keep 2.3-2.5 tokens per chat check, and the check's fixed cost is ~75-85 % of a chat check. So a drafter that never missed would still reach only ~105-115 tok/s of chat decode at today's check cost [P]. Against Strata on the same machine, our chat decode is about level, and Strata's first token wins every whole-request number up to 256 generated tokens. We also continued the chat head overnight on the model's own chat features. A memory guard stopped capture at 0.10M tokens, and the head kept x1.014 on held-out chat: a KILL by its own line, at 8 % of its planned data [K].

We also hit a training pitfall. MLX runs fp32 matmuls as TF32 unless told otherwise, which moved our drafter's training losses by 1.3-3.5 % against a float32 port.

Running a 125B mixture-of-experts model on a 64 GB Mac: the engine package

Kamen Levi

9 November 2026

This paper documents the engine package behind [MAIN]. The engine runs Qwen3.8-Flash-Next, a 125B mixture-of-experts model, on one MacBook Pro (M5 Pro, 18-core CPU, 20-core GPU, 64 GB, 2 TB SSD): the experts are stored once on the internal SSD, and each layer keeps a fixed pool of them in memory. It installs from a clean clone (tested on one machine) and ships six configurations: exact, lossy and chat, each at a 45 GB and a 32 GB memory line, labelled as [MAIN] section 2 defines. Package 1.1.0 adds a 24 GB line: one gated lossy configuration, under the names lossy and chat (section 2; [SMALL-RAM]). It also adds a chat in the terminal and a local server for chat apps in two common API formats, text only (section 4). At 45 GB, the exact configuration, bit-identical to one-token greedy decoding under our reference NAX6-N, decodes 37.1-101.4 tok/s; the shipped lossy one, which scored the same as the exact reference on the model card's IFBench, GPQA Diamond and LiveCodeBench v6 in both orders, decodes 45.2-164.0 tok/s; both stay at a lifetime peak of 41.7-43.2 GB, plus a 7.45 GB expert tier that macOS may reclaim [K, section 2]. Where we lose: on the same machine and prompts, Strata's first token (0.5-1.0 s) beats ours (2.7-4.6 s; 2.26 s on chat as shipped), so up to 256 generated tokens Strata wins or ties every whole-request number [K, section 2]. The code is GPL-3; the model's weights keep their own licence (section 7).

A 125B mixture-of-experts model in 32, 24 and 12 GB of memory on one Mac

Kamen Levi

11 November 2026

We run Qwen3.8-Flash-Next, a 125B mixture-of-experts model (48 layers, 512 routed experts per layer, 10 per token), on one MacBook Pro (M5 Pro, 18-core CPU, 20-core GPU, 64 GB, 2 TB SSD). Experts are streamed from the internal SSD. On our speed sets the process's own lifetime peak footprint stays under 32, 24 or 12 GB, except for one 520-token math prompt at 24 GB (24.01 GB); longer prompts go over the 24 and 32 GB lines (section 6.3).

At 12 GB a block drafter's 15 drafts are checked by the 12 GB model in calls of 2-4 rows. Experts are stored at about 3.2 bits per weight.

  • The loop is verified: every committed token is the model's greedy argmax at its computed width. It is not bit-identical to one-token decoding.
  • It is deterministic and independent of the cache contents and of the prompt order.
  • On 12 HumanEval + 12 MBPP it runs 18.3 tok/s whole decode, 13.1 tok/s whole request and 4.3 s first token, at an 11.14 GB peak. On chat (12 IFEval) it runs 12.8 / 10.1 tok/s and 2.9 s, at 10.96 GB [K].

At 24 GB a lossy configuration ([MAIN]'s L + COH with 141 + 26 expert slots per layer and checks of at most 13 rows) runs 130.3 tok/s whole decode, 40.8 tok/s whole request and 3.54 s first token on 12 HumanEval, at a 23.82 GB peak [K].

  • It passed its quality gate: the same scores as the exact reference on IFBench, GPQA Diamond and LiveCodeBench v6, in both orders [K].
  • In our runs it stayed under 24 GB on prompts of up to about 500 tokens, with little room: a 448-token prompt reached 23.954 GB and a 520-token one 24.01 GB. Longer prompts use more: about 25.0 GB at 3K tokens, 25.8 GB at 10K and 28.2 GB at 30K [K].
  • On 12 MBPP it runs 91.2 / 26.1 tok/s and 3.3 s, on chat (12 IFEval) 30.6 / 18.1 tok/s and 3.1 s, and on 12 math prompts 40.8 / 27.4 tok/s and 2.9 s [K].
  • An older exact configuration (exact to its own stock-kernel reference) runs 41.1 tok/s whole decode on 12 HumanEval and 37.4 on 12 MBPP, at a 23.86 GB peak [K]. Its whole request and first token were not recorded.

At 32 GB, on chat (12 IFEval) [K]:

  • The exact configuration, token-identical to the 45 GB reference on 12 / 12 prompts, runs 33.8 tok/s whole decode, 19.8 whole request and 2.8 s first token, at a 30.62 GB peak.
  • A lossy chat configuration, not gated at 32 GB, runs 51.3 / 22.5 / 3.0 s at 31.22 GB.

Where we lose.

  • Strata (MIT; github.com/Niko1221/Strata), an open-source engine for this model, cannot run at 12 GB: its smallest setting peaks at 14.19 GB.
  • But at that setting its chat beats ours: 14.4 vs 12.8 tok/s decode, 11.2 vs 10.1 whole request, 2.8 vs 2.9 s first token.
  • At 24 GB, on the same prompts, its first token (1.3-2.3 s) is ahead of ours (2.9-3.5 s). On chat its whole request is also ahead (20.3-21.0 vs 18.1 tok/s), and our decode leads only 1.1x. On code and math our whole decode (40.8-130.3 vs 26.1-28.0 tok/s) and whole request (26.1-40.8 vs 20.3-23.0) lead.
  • Our 24 GB configuration can go over 24 GB on prompts longer than about 500 tokens: 25.0 GB at 3K tokens, 28.2 GB at 30K. On six long gate prompts (726-2,801 tokens), 256-token prompt-pass chunks kept it under, but cost 2.0-2.9x the first token and changed the tokens, so its gate does not cover them.
  • Our 12 GB first token is 2.9-4.3 s, because the prompt pass streams experts from the SSD: about 22 GB for a 63-token prompt.
  • Our 12 GB lossy two-pass mode failed its HumanEval+ gate at every threshold tried.

Two early headlines were wrong. "12 GB, 40.8 tok/s exact" was a lossy arm, and "the likely first at <= 12 GB, exact" falls with it.

We measure where a 12 GB verify call spends its time. Levers priced and not yet run:

  • two-row verify calls on chat [P: +10-15 %]
  • Strata's next-layer read-ahead
  • a lossy 24 GB chat route that reads 81.7 MB of SSD per kept token instead of 210.2 [P]
