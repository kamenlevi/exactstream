## Abstract

We run a 125B-parameter mixture-of-experts model (Qwen3.8-Flash-Next: 48 layers, 512 routed experts per layer, 10 per
token) on one MacBook Pro (M5 Pro, 18-core CPU, 20-core GPU, 64 GB, 2 TB SSD), with the routed experts streamed from the internal SSD at about 3.2
bits per weight and the process's lifetime peak at most 45 GB (plus a 7.45 GB expert tier that macOS may reclaim). The
pieces are mostly published; this paper is about how they depend
on each other and what each adds. Speculative decoding pays one pass of the large model for up to 16 drafted tokens, but
it is exact only if that pass computes the same bits as one-token decoding. Batch-invariant kernels on the GPU and its
matrix units make it so, and we audit it on every run (96 of 96 code runs, 24 of 24 chat and 24 of 24 math runs
token-identical to one-token greedy decoding). Exactness then turns every later optimisation into a test of token
equality that takes minutes: the levers of the streamed engine, such as per-layer expert pools and expert reads
hidden behind the GPU's work, were each adopted that way. On top of the exact engine, a lossy layer
lets drafted rows of code answers skip cold experts and run 3-bit parts, and ships only if it scores the same as the
exact reference on the model's benchmarks.

The exact configuration decodes 101.4 tok/s on 12 HumanEval prompts, 70.4 on 12 MBPP, 77.9 on 12 math and 37.1 on 12
chat (IFEval) prompts, at a lifetime peak of 41.7-42.5 GB [K]. The lossy configuration we ship scores the same as the
exact reference on the model card's IFBench, GPQA Diamond and LiveCodeBench v6 in both prompt orders, and decodes 164.0,
112.7, 78.9 and 45.2 tok/s on the same sets at 42.4-43.2 GB [K]. Our first token is slow, a median of 2.7-4.6 s on these
short prompts: on the same machine and prompts the open engine Strata's takes 0.54-1.01 s, and Strata wins or ties every
whole request of up to 256 tokens, although our lossy decode is 2.5-3.5x its decode on code [K]. None of the methods is
specific to this model's layers; we tested one model on one machine. Section 12 describes each finding that, to our
knowledge, has not been published before, with its evidence, what it gives and its limits, including one idea that did
not pay off. We also report negative results, including three
headline numbers we corrected or withdrew.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The setting

- **The model.** Qwen3.8-Flash-Next (arXiv 2608.30320) is an open-weight mixture-of-experts model with a hybrid trunk: 36
  linear-attention (Gated DeltaNet, "GDN") layers and 12 full-attention layers, four residual streams joined by low-rank
  hyper-connection (HC) mixers, one shared expert and 512 routed experts per layer with 10 routed per token, and a
  multi-token-prediction (MTP) head. It has about 125B parameters in total (section 4.1).
- **The machine.** One MacBook Pro, M5 Pro, 64 GB of unified memory, internal SSD. All numbers are for one user, greedy
  decoding.
- **The problem.** At MLX 4-bit the routed experts alone are 67.9 GB [K, BM:FINDINGS.md section 1], so they cannot all
  stay in memory. A token reads about 2.46 GB of dense weights and 1.33 GB of routed experts [K, same]. Decoding one token
  at a time pays a full pass over the dense trunk per token.
- **Speculative decoding** pays that pass once per check: a small drafter proposes a block of tokens and one pass of the
  large model verifies up to 16 of them.
- **Why exactness is not free.** A verify pass computes 16 positions in one batched call; one-token decoding computes each
  in a 1-row call. On stock kernels those are different programs with different rounding: in our first screen all 16
  rows differed from one-token decoding at layer 0 (section 3). A greedy decoder then drifts at near-ties, and
  "speculative decoding is lossless" stops being true.

### 1.2 The idea: known pieces that need each other

The pieces build on published work; section 8 credits each one and says which part is ours, and the prior art is in
Appendix I. Section 12 describes the findings of this work that, to our
knowledge, have not been published before. What this paper adds is the order in which they depend on each other, and that they still work, exactly,
when combined. Section 7 gives each step's measured effect, and says where a step was never measured alone.

1. **Exact checks.** Speculative decoding verifies up to 16 drafted tokens in one pass, but greedy decoding is unchanged
   only if that pass computes the same bits as 16 one-token steps. Batch-invariant kernels make it so (section 3;
   [KERNELS]).
2. **Optimisation by token equality.** With an exact reference, a change to the engine is accepted when every token
   equals the reference on a handful of prompts, in minutes, with no quality argument (section 3.3). Every lever of the
   45 GB engine was adopted that way (section 4.4).
3. **A store that fits.** The routed experts (120.8B parameters) live on the SSD as aligned records with a 3-bit part and
   a 4th-bit sidecar; each layer keeps a pool of them in memory, and a token's two top-ranked experts read both parts
   while the other eight read the 3-bit part (sections 4.1-4.2). The check loop is what makes streaming affordable: a
   check pays the SSD reads for the union of its rows' experts once, and every token it keeps shares them.
4. **Hiding the reads.** Expert ids stay on the GPU, and resident experts compute while missed ones load (section 4.4).
   The experts' outputs are still summed in a fixed order, so both changes keep every token equal and were accepted as
   in step 2.
5. **A certified lossy layer.** On drafted rows of code answers the engine skips cold lower-ranked experts and runs the
   3-bit part of the rest, and a few reads are made at lower precision everywhere (section 5). The check loop says which
   rows are drafted; the layer ships only if it scores the same as the exact reference of step 1 on the model's
   benchmarks.
6. **Prefill from the same store.** A short prompt can build its experts from the copies already in memory and read only
   what is missing (PF-D, shipped for chat); a long prompt can run each layer over the whole prompt, so that each expert
   is read about once (built, not shipped; section 4.5).

### 1.3 What carries to other models

None of the methods depends on this model's linear-attention or hyper-connection layers (section 8). The exact check needs
batch-invariant kernels for the model's operations; the store, the loader and the lossy row rules need a mixture of
experts with a drafter. The design is meant as an engine for such models in general, but we tested one model on one
machine, and that is the limit of what this paper shows.

### 1.4 Contributions

1. **Exact speculative checks at 125B on Apple silicon (section 3; kernels in [KERNELS]).** A 16-row check that equals 16
   one-token steps bit for bit, audited on every measured run, and a rule for changing the reference.
2. **An engine built by token equality (sections 4 and 7).** An SSD-streamed store, cache and loader within a 45 GB
   memory line, each lever accepted by token equality, and an account of what each piece adds on top of the others and
   which steps were never measured alone.
3. **A lossy configuration certified on the model's benchmarks (section 5; method in [EVAL]).** The same scores as the
   exact reference on HumanEval+, MBPP, math and IFEval, and, for the configuration we ship, on the model card's
   IFBench, GPQA Diamond and LiveCodeBench v6, in both prompt orders.
4. **Methods that carry to other MoE models (section 8), a short comparison with other engines (section 9), and negative
   results (section 10) and the path to more speed (section 11).**
5. **New findings (section 12).** Findings that, to our knowledge, have not been published before, each with its
   evidence, what it gives, what is measured and what is still a hypothesis, and how it could be improved.

### 1.5 What we do not claim

- **Not the fastest engine for this model.** Our first token is 2.7-7.0x slower than Strata's on the same machine, and
  engines with lower-bit files or on larger Macs report a faster decode or prefill (self-reported; section 9). Our decode
  leads on code and math, slightly on chat in the lossy mode, and by a wide margin only in the lossy mode on code.
- **Not new pieces.** Every lever in the measured configurations builds on published work, credited in section 8 and
  Appendix I; the findings that are, to our knowledge, new are in section 12. What we add is making them work together, exactly, at this
  size on this hardware, and measuring what each adds. To our knowledge no published engine for this model combines all
  of them; Strata and Sushi combine several (section 9).
- **Not exact to the original 4-bit weights.** "Exact" always names a reference (section 2); ours runs ranks 0-1 of each
  token's experts at 4 bits and ranks 2-9 at 3 bits.

### 1.6 The companion papers

[KERNELS] has the batch-invariant kernels and the MLX findings; [EVAL] the certification method and its pitfalls;
[DRAFTER] the drafter work, including what failed; [RELEASE] the engine package (GPL-3). The 32, 24 and 12 GB lines are
the subject of a forthcoming companion paper ([SMALL-RAM], by 11 November 2026); no number in this paper depends on it.
This paper states each companion's results in one sentence where it needs them.

## 2. Definitions and how we measure

- **Exact (to R).** The output tokens, and the logits row behind every kept token, are bit-identical to one-token greedy
  decoding under reference R, a fixed set of weights, kernels and call shapes. Changing a kernel changes rounding and
  makes a new reference, which is accepted once on task scores (section 3.4). Our 45 GB reference is NAX6-N.
- **Lossy, same scores on <sets>.** A configuration that changes which computation a row runs, accepted only after its
  pre-registered same-scores gate against its exact reference on the named known benchmarks, in two prompt orders. Never
  "same quality".
- **Lossy, gate open** (a gate is running or undecided) and **lossy, ungated** (no gate). Every configuration in this paper
  carries one of these labels.
- **Speed** comes as a triple for a named prompt set:
  - whole decode = generated tokens / decode wall time (prefill excluded, every check included);
  - whole request = generated tokens / (first token time + decode wall time);
  - first token = request start to the first generated token, including prefill and setup (median and range);
  - plus the lifetime peak memory footprint of the process (ri_lifetime_max_phys_footprint, which counts the GPU's buffers),
    in GB of 10^9 bytes; the 45 GB line is 41.9 GiB [K, ES:evalsuite/decode.py, footprint_peak_gb];
  - not counted in it: a 7.45 GB volatile expert tier (64 / 32 slots per base / sidecar pool), purgeable memory that
    macOS reclaims before it compresses or swaps; every run log behind Table 6.1 states it [K, e.g.
    BM:data/rel/rli_160.txt line 7].
- **Aggregation.** Pooled over prompts and passes (total tokens / total time). The engine's run reports quote a geometric
  mean over prompts ("geo"), 0-8 % higher on our sets; both are in Appendix E.
- **Workloads.** 12 HumanEval and 12 MBPP prompts (the same ids in every run), 12 IFEval prompts as our chat set and 12 math
  (MATH-500) prompts; greedy, at most 256 new tokens, EOS stop. HumanEval flatters: 67.8 % of the generated tokens on our 12
  HumanEval prompts copy the prompt's own signature and docstring [K, F:lossy350-thinking-hour], which drafting accepts
  cheaply. We never quote HumanEval without MBPP or chat beside it.
- **The machine during runs.** Other jobs were stopped through a GPU lock ("quiet"); a run's report lists pauses for heat
  and drops any request that overlaps one.
- **Marks.** [K] measured, [P] priced (arithmetic or a CPU replay on measured data), [G] judgement. Every number names its
  source (a run record, report or result file); Appendix C maps the keys to paths.
- **The memory line.** "45 GB" means the run's lifetime peak is at most 45.0 GB. It is the engine's own footprint on a 64
  GB machine, not a limit the operating system enforces.

## 3. Exactness (details in [KERNELS])

### 3.1 References

| reference | what it is | status |
|---|---|---|
| REF2 | batch-invariant kernel set: every matmul, HC read and attention call padded to 16 rows, routed experts on one 16-row tile kernel, a load-time self-test | adopted 28 Oct (T1210) |
| REF2b | REF2 + attention key buckets past 512 keys, attention classes below 128 keys, the commit phase and slot call | adopted 31 Oct (T1219) |
| REF2-T | REF2b on rank-tiered experts: ranks 0-1 at 4 bits, ranks 2-9 at 3 bits | 1 Nov (T1214, T307) |
| NAX6-N | REF2-T with the routed experts on a matrix-unit kernel over records in the kernel's native layout | certified 5 Nov; the 45 GB exact reference (EV:RESULT_nax6_2026-11-05) |

The first screen compared 16 one-token steps with one 16-row verify on identical caches, layer by layer: with the shipped
kernels all 16 rows diverged at layer 0, because the framework picks a different matmul kernel by row count; attention,
long context (past 512 keys) and short context (below 128 keys) broke next [K, LT:'R-40 REF2'; T1225; T1208; T1218]. The
fixes and the kernels are [KERNELS]'s subject.

### 3.2 The audits

- **The chat failure and its fix.** A one-call path for chat checks made exact chat nondeterministic (3 of 12 prompts
  equal). Coherent reads in the expert kernel restored 12 of 12 at no cost (F:2026-11-08_0750); [KERNELS] has the
  mechanism.
- **The audit is part of every run.** Each exact row of section 6 carries its own token-identity count. It is how two bugs
  were caught that speed numbers alone hide: a fast path that never engaged (T18) and a kernel that was silently bypassed
  (F:flat2, caveat).

### 3.3 What exactness buys

Once a reference exists, an optimisation is accepted by token equality on a handful of prompts, in minutes, with no
quality argument. Every lever of section 4.4 was accepted this way. It has a price: the batch-invariant one-token path
ran at x0.75 of stock kernels (11.65 vs 15.50 tok/s over 210 rows, row time with prefill included; information only)
[K, EV:RESULT_ref2_2026-10-28; [KERNELS] R31].

### 3.4 Changing the reference: certified once, on scores

A new reference moves bits, so it is judged on tasks, once, by a rule fixed in advance ("decision 4"): on each of three
sets (math, n 50; IFEval, n 100, strict prompt level; HumanEval+, n 60, pass@1) the paired score difference must be above
-5 points and the exact McNemar p at least 0.05.

| new reference vs old | math | IFEval | HumanEval+ | verdict | source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| REF2 vs the previous reference | +8.0 | -3.0 | 0.0 | PASS | EV:RESULT_ref2_2026-10-28 |
| REF2b vs the previous reference | +6.0 | -3.0 | 0.0 | PASS | T1219 |
| REF2-T vs REF2b | 0.0 | +3.0 | +3.3 | PASS | T307 |
| NAX6 vs REF2-T | 0.0 | -3.0 (p 0.375) | -3.3 (p 0.50) | PASS | EV:RESULT_nax6_2026-11-05 |

These are same-score tests on 210 items, not proofs of equal quality: pooled over the NAX6 sets, NAX6 alone is right on 2
items and REF2-T alone on 7 (two-sided p 0.18). A further kernel bundle (REF2c-T) failed the rule on IFEval (-5.0) and was
not adopted. The gaps chain: the certified lossy config of section 5 is -8.33 points on the HumanEval+ core set against REF2-T, two
references back (forward order, p 0.0625), which no gate of its own measured [K, F:2026-11-08_0455; information].

## 4. The 45 GB system

### 4.1 Model, store and hardware

| part | parameters | notes |
|---|---|---|
| routed experts | 120.8 B | 48 layers x 512 experts, top-10; one expert = gate/up [1280 x 2560] + down [2560 x 640] |
| linear attention (GDN) | 2.09 B | 36 of 48 layers |
| full attention + indexer | 0.62 B | 12 layers, 24 query heads, 2 key/value heads, head dim 256 |
| hyper-connections | 0.64 B | 4 residual streams, low-rank mixers |
| embedding / output head / shared expert / router | 0.64 / 0.64 / 0.24 / 0.06 B | one shared expert per layer |
| MTP head | 2.61 B | one layer with its own 512 experts |
| n-gram tables | 51.2 B | layer 1 only; 16 rows read per token, from the SSD |

Source: BM:FINDINGS.md section 1 (config and safetensors headers). **Active per token [P, arithmetic on this table]:**
10 of 512 routed experts in each of 48 layers (480 x 4.92 M = 2.36 B), attention 2.71 B, hyper-connections 0.64 B, the
shared expert and router 0.30 B: 6.0 B, or 6.65 B with the output head. The 125.7 B total counts the rows above except
the MTP head, the vision tower and the n-gram tables (16 rows of 160 values read per token); with them the checkpoint
stores 180 B, the size some engines quote for this model (Kapitsa, Mference; Appendix H). Our engine runs this architecture
through the model code of MTPLX by Youssof Altoukhi (Apache-2.0; github.com/youssofal/mtplx); the store, the kernels and
the check loop are ours.

- **Records.** Each expert is one contiguous, 16 KB-aligned record, read with one call that bypasses the page cache
  (F_NOCACHE): an MSB part (the 3 most significant bits of every weight with scales and biases) and an LSB sidecar (the 4th
  bit). The 4-bit weights are rebuilt bit-exactly from the two parts; the store is 68.5 GB on disk (48 layer files; 2.79 MB per expert record) [K, BM:models/bare/records3, the converter's output; not in the public snapshot]. For the
  matrix-unit kernel the records are stored in the kernel's own fragment order, with scales packed to 11 bits (bitwise;
  T527, T334).
- **Rank tiers.** A token's two highest-scoring experts (ranks 0-1) run at 4 bits and read both parts; ranks 2-9 run from
  the MSB part alone. Expert bytes fall about 20 % (T307).
- **The SSD.** Cold 2.77 MB reads reach 14.0 GB/s at queue depth 16 [K, BM:FINDINGS.md section 2]. In the engine the SSD
  time per check follows 10-16 ms plus 0.15-0.17 ms per missed expert [K, T288].

### 4.2 Expert cache and loader

- Each layer has a pool of expert slots in GPU memory; C 256 means 256 slots per layer on average, with per-layer sizes
  at equal memory (-17.2 % SSD bytes per kept token and +8.3 % tok/s, exact; T12, adapted from AdapMoE). Eviction is LRU.
- All misses of a layer load in parallel, aligned, straight into slot memory (T7; M-29). Part of the pool is a volatile
  tier the operating system may reclaim (T1088).
- **The wired limit.** MLX's wired-memory collector unwired the expert pools during prefill, and each prompt's first check
  re-wired about 31.8 GB, blocking for about 250 ms. One call after load (mx.set_wired_limit, published practice in
  mlx-lm) removes it, bitwise in the exact mode: +25.1 % whole decode, pooled, on the lossy config (144.1 -> 180.1 tok/s,
  12 HumanEval, two passes each; the notes give geo means, 142.2 -> 181.2) [K, BM:data/r57/s6{b,w}{1,2}_256_runs.json;
  F:2026-11-07_0900, F:2026-11-07_0945].

### 4.3 The check loop

1. **Draft.** A block drafter (our fine-tune of DFlash2 by Inco AI, Apache-2.0; [DRAFTER], Appendix I) proposes up to 16 positions in
one pass; chat uses the model's own MTP head,
   chosen by task (T782). Drafts are kept while the running product of the drafter's confidences stays at or
   above 0.3 (T309).
2. **Verify.** One padded 16-row pass of the 125B model checks the drafts; they are accepted up to the first mismatch, and
   the row after the last accepted draft gives one more token (the bonus row).
3. **Commit.** The recurrent and attention states advance by the kept rows only.

Tokens kept per check decide the speed and depend on the workload: 10.7 per check on HumanEval, 6.0-6.1 on MBPP, 6.5 on
math and 2.5 on chat (kept tokens over checks, pooled) [K, F:2026-11-07_1735; F:2026-11-07_2355]. Chat checks are short because the drafter is unsure, and
their cost is the low-row check's fixed cost (about 51 ms exact, 40 ms lossy at 1-2 rows, against a memory floor near
15-20 ms) [K, F:2026-11-07_2355].

### 4.4 The exact default and its levers

| confidence cut at 0.3 | section 4.3 | +19 % | T309 |
| out-of-order experts | resident experts compute first, missed ones as their reads land, in a fixed bitwise order | +13 to +18.7 % | T14, T490 |
| per-layer pool sizes | section 4.2 | +8.3 % | M-17 |
| drafter by task | MTP head on chat | chat +24.5 % (time-weighted) on 19 fresh chat prompts | T782 |
| SSD keepalive | section 8.1 | about -3 ms per check | BM:docs/NIGHT_1104.md:37 |
| matrix-unit experts on native records (NAX6-N) | section 3.1 | +6.2 % whole decode, pooled (44.74 vs 42.12 tok/s, 13 prompts, C 192; +6.7 % on the steady rate, information only) | LP:data/natfri/natfri_pooled.txt; LL, 'NATIVE-FRI run 2' |
| cache keep | the loop no longer clears the kernels' per-weight caches before every check | -7.2 to -8.0 ms per check; x1.06 whole decode on the lossy config, pooled (x1.10 on the steady rate, information only); tokens equal | F:2026-11-07_0144; BM:data/r57/sc{b,k}{1,2}_256_runs.json |

| the exact-safe bundle (EB) | a bundle of kernel changes (seams a-e, DQ1; [KERNELS]), bitwise | +3.7 % HumanEval, +2.9 % MBPP (pooled) | F:2026-11-07_1735; Appendix E |
| coherent reads | GPU reads of loader-written memory made coherent | exact chat 3 / 12 -> 12 / 12, no cost | F:2026-11-08_0750 |

### 4.5 Prefill

Today's prefill runs the prompt in 1,024-row chunks and streams each chunk's experts from the SSD, so a long prompt reads
the expert store several times (430 GB for a 7.9K-token prompt). Layer-major prefill runs each layer over the whole
prompt and reads each expert about once (68 GB), exactly: 1.9-3.4x faster at 1.6K-7.9K tokens (section 6.2) [K,
F:2026-11-08_0800]. On short prompts the first token is still 2.7-4.6 s (section 6.3), except on chat with PF-D.

PF-D builds a short prompt's routed experts from the copies already in memory and reads only the missing parts from the
SSD, instead of re-reading every routed expert (20.4 -> 7.5-9.6 GB per chat prompt [K]); its version 4 also reads the
next layer's missing experts during the current layer. Both follow Strata's prompt-path design (read only what memory
lacks; read the next layer's misses during this layer); no Strata code is included ([RELEASE] section 7). PF-D is
exact: on the shipped chat configuration its tokens (12 / 12) and logits hashes (721 / 721) equal the same configuration
without it, and a check arm on the exact configuration (16 tokens per prompt) found all 87,912 staged rows equal to the
reference rebuild and its tokens equal to one-token decoding on 12 / 12 prompts [K, BM:data/pfd/pfd_report_0009.txt].
It ships on for chat at 45 GB only (Table 6.1c); every other row of section 6 was measured without it, and it was not
run at 32 GB.

## 5. The lossy configuration and its certificate (method in [EVAL])

### 5.1 What it changes

The certified configuration (snapshot d8eb2e4; its full switch list is `BM:data/stack/cert_env.txt`) keeps the check loop
and changes what some rows compute. Grouped by effect:

- **Lossy on code sets only (HumanEval, MBPP):** QOM-KEEP: on a drafted row, ranks 0-1 always run; a rank 2-9 expert runs
  only if the check loads it anyway or it is already resident, and otherwise drops out at weight 0, with no rerouting; the
  kept experts of drafted rows run from the 3-bit part (U3). Section 8.2 has the evidence.
- **Lossy everywhere (bits change on every set):** 8-bit hyper-connection mixers read in one pass on the matrix units
  (HC8); a split of K for narrow dense matrix-vector calls (DN-KS, rounding-level); single-call attention; small weights in
  the native layout; the matrix-unit expert form of the above. Single-call attention is bitwise below 512 keys.
- **Acceptance:** exact everywhere, except inside '#' comments of code answers, where a draft ranked first or second with at
  least a quarter of the top token's probability is kept (section 8.3).
- **Drafting:** one extra draft position so that the bonus row is gated too (DRAFT17); 17-32-row drafter projections on the
  matrix units; a drafter head over a reduced vocabulary; a copy check that continues a verbatim copy of the prompt in the
  draft (2,888 copied tokens accepted on 12 HumanEval prompts; F:2026-11-07_0025). It only drafts: the longest 8-16-token
  match in the committed context replaces drafts while a per-session duel says the copy wins at least 60 % of its
  disagreements with the drafter; the check still decides every token; a row whose draft the copy changed never counts as
  sure for the lossy row rules; it is off on math [K, code read: BM:scripts/r57_screen.py, R57_COPY=g2].
- **Exact plumbing:** lazy GDN commit, submitting work every two layers, the cache keep and the wired limit.

### 6.3 Where a request's time goes

- On the four 12-prompt sets the first token takes 43-80 % of a whole request's wall time [P on K: Table 6.1, e.g.
  HumanEval lossy as shipped 218 tokens per task at 164.0 tok/s = 1.33 s of decode against 6.03 s per request; exact chat 3.79 of 6.77
  s is decode]. Decode is no longer the bottleneck at these answer lengths; the first token is.
- Why it is slow on short prompts: a chat prompt's prefill reads about 20 GB of experts, because every prompt token routes
  10 experts in each of 48 layers, and it re-reads experts that are already resident; first token = 0.32 s + GB / 7.33 GB/s
  [P: a least-squares fit on the 12 CT1 rows of BM:data/pfb/pfb_report.txt; section 11].
- PF-D (section 4.5) cuts the shipped chat configuration's read from 20.4 to 9.6 GB per prompt and its first token from
  2.91 to 2.26 s; whole request rises from 24.6 to 28.8 tok/s and decode is unchanged (50.3 / 50.7) [K, Table 6.1c]. The
  fit above gives 1.63 s for 9.6 GB, so with PF-D the reads alone no longer set the first token [P on K].
- What it costs a user is section 9: against an engine with a 0.5-1.0 s first token, a decode up to 3.5x faster (on code)
  does not pay back within 256 tokens.

## 7. How the pieces build on each other

Steps 1-5 are in the order in which each needs the ones before it; steps 6 and 7 apply to any configuration. Each effect
was measured when its piece was adopted, on the engine of that day and in that run's own metric (section 4.4), so the
effects do not add up to Table 6.1, and we give no waterfall. No run removes one piece at a time from the final
configuration.

| step | piece | what it does | what later steps need from it | measured effect | source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | batch-invariant kernels (REF2 to NAX6-N; section 3) | a 16-row check computes the same bits as 16 one-token steps | a reference against which any later change is a token-equality test | exactness, audited on every run (section 3.2); the one-token path ran at x0.75 of stock kernels (information only) | EV:RESULT_ref2_2026-10-28; EV:RESULT_nax6_2026-11-05 |
| 2 | the check loop: a block drafter cut at confidence 0.3, the MTP head on chat (section 4.3) | one pass of the 125B model keeps 2.5-10.7 tokens per check, by task | a pass whose dense and SSD costs every kept token shares; drafted rows that step 5 can treat differently | confidence cut +19 %; MTP head on chat +24.5 % (time-weighted, 19 chat prompts); no A/B against one-token decoding on the final engine | T309; T782; F:2026-11-07_1735; F:2026-11-07_2355 |
| 3 | the streamed store: aligned records, per-layer pools, rank tiers (sections 4.1-4.2) | the experts fit a 45 GB line; a check reads each missing expert once for all its rows | a loader whose reads can overlap the GPU's work | per-layer pool sizes -17.2 % SSD bytes per kept token, +8.3 % tok/s, exact; rank tiers about -20 % expert bytes (a new reference, certified on scores) | T12; T307 |

| 5 | the lossy layer (CERT; section 5) | drafted rows of code answers skip cold lower-ranked experts and run 3-bit parts; some reads at lower precision everywhere | - | CERT 171-175 tok/s on HumanEval and 111-114 on MBPP, against 101.4 and 70.4 for the exact default; the two differ in more than the lossy parts (section 5.1), so this is not an A/B of the layer | Tables 6.1a and 6.1b; Appendix E |
| 6 | per-check fixed costs: cache keep, wired limit, matrix-unit experts, the exact-safe bundle, coherent reads, the 1-row check (section 4.4) | removes per-check clears and a ~250 ms first-check stall; faster kernels; coherent reads make exact chat deterministic | - | cache keep -7.2 to -8.0 ms per check; wired limit +25.1 % whole decode (lossy configuration); matrix-unit experts on native records (NAX6-N, later the reference) +6.2 %; the bundle +3.7 % and +2.9 % on code; coherent reads 3 / 12 -> 12 / 12 exact chat prompts; the 1-row check +1 % exact chat, +4.2 % lossy chat | section 4.4 |
| 7 | prefill from the same store (section 4.5) | PF-D builds a short prompt's experts from the copies in memory; layer-major prefill reads each expert about once | - | PF-D: chat as shipped, first token 2.91 -> 2.26 s, exact; layer-major 1.9-3.4x at 1.6K-7.9K tokens, exact (built, not shipped) | BM:data/pfd/pfd_report_0009.txt; F:2026-11-08_0800 |

- **Why exactness comes first.** The levers of steps 2-4 and 6 were each accepted by token equality, in minutes; rank
  tiers, which change bits on purpose, were certified once as a new reference. Without the reference of step 1, each
  lever would have needed a benchmark gate, as the lossy layer does (sections 3.3-3.4).
- **Why the check loop makes the store affordable.** A check's SSD time follows 10-16 ms plus 0.15-0.17 ms per missed
  expert [K, T288], paid once per check and shared by the tokens it keeps: 10.7 per check on HumanEval, 6.0-6.1 on MBPP,
  6.5 on math and 2.5 on chat [K, F:2026-11-07_1735; F:2026-11-07_2355]. Chat keeps few tokens per check, so its cost
  is the check's fixed cost (about 51 ms exact at 1-2 rows) [K, F:2026-11-07_2355], which is why the per-check costs of
  step 6 matter most on chat.
- **Why the lossy layer needs the others.** It decides per drafted row (step 2), reads the 3-bit part that the store
  already keeps separately (step 3), and is certified against the exact reference (step 1). It pays most on code, where
  its expert rules apply: the lossy configuration we ship decodes 164.0 tok/s on HumanEval and 112.7 on MBPP against
  101.4 and 70.4 for the exact default, 78.9 against 77.9 on math, where those rules are off, and 45.2 against 37.1 on
  chat [K, Tables 6.1a and 6.1b].
- **Where pieces do not compose.** Exact pieces compose by construction, and the combination is audited on every run
  (section 3.2). Lossy pieces need not: on chat, relaxed acceptance alone scored IFEval 87 and 8-bit hyper-connection
  mixers alone 89, both together 84, against 90 for the reference (section 10.6). This is why the lossy layer is gated
  as one configuration, not part by part.

### 8.1 The SSD wake penalty and the keepalive

- **Builds on.** An NVMe drive enters an idle power state after an idle time and pays a published exit latency on the
  next request (Microsoft's NVMe power-management guidance for StorNVMe); keeping a drive awake with periodic I/O is
  old practice (for example Torpid, which writes a small file every 2.5 s); waking a device ahead of predicted I/O is
  published (HotOS '15). What we found on this MacBook Pro is in section 12.1.
- **Why it works [G].** The penalty depends on the idle gap and not on CPU load (below), so it sits in the SSD path,
  consistent with an idle timer in the drive or its link. A read every 270 us or sooner keeps that timer from expiring;
  a single read about 200 us before a burst pays the wake early, off the critical path.
- **Finding.** On this machine's internal SSD, the first read after an idle gap is slower. After gaps of 0.25-1 ms it
  costs +50-65 us per burst; after 20 ms, +100-120 us [K, T93]. A streamed MoE produces exactly such gaps: one burst
  of expert reads per layer, with GPU work between bursts. If every layer pays, that is about 3 ms per check at 45 GB
  [P, T93].
- **A per-call decomposition** puts the fixed cost of one loader call at 187 us of the engine's 283 us: first byte
  about 119 us, wake 44, dispatch 8, and about 96 us outside the I/O path [K, T97].
- **Fix.** A trickle of 4 KB reads keeps the drive awake. A real spacing of at most 270 us removes the whole +44 us;
  400 us or more leaves half [K, T44]. A busy CPU core does nothing, so the effect is in the SSD path [K, T93].
- **In the engine:** a 50 us keepalive, about -3 ms per check on clean prompts [K, BM:docs/NIGHT_1104.md:37], adopted
  in the exact default as part of the +12.1 % bundle (T10). No isolated A/B of the keepalive exists on the final
  engine (the engine's owner, 6 Nov), so its share of the bundle is known only from that figure.
- **A cheaper form** is a single 4 KB read about 200 us before a predicted burst. It removes 97.5 % of the penalty in
  microbenchmarks (100 us ahead is too late, 400 us too early), and 87-94 % at the engine's real gaps [K, T47, T98].
  It is not live.
- **What does not work:** pinging the record's own first block to pre-warm it (+0.6 to +3.2 us, T202); splitting one
  record into parallel reads (each piece pays its own request latency, T199); the page cache (+49 us per call, T201).

### 8.2 Three-bit top-ranked experts on rows the drafter is sure of (as shipped: on every drafted row)

- **Background.** Ranks 0-1 at 4 bits are load-bearing: all ranks at 3 bits fails both the divergence gate and the
  same-scores test (`TRIED_SHORT.md`; EV:RESULT_relax_2026-11-05, T3ALL: HumanEval+ 88.3 vs 95.0).
- **The signal.** On rows that verify a draft the drafter was confident about, the LSB sidecar is not read for
  experts that only such rows use. The rationale [G]: on those rows the large model's top token rarely depends on
  the 4th bit of its top experts. The evidence is the flip rates below.
- **Evidence [K, decision map].** With the union rule at certainty 0.9: 1.19 / 5.06 / 3.24 % flips on full 16-row
  checks, under the lines (T103). On the QOM form at certainty 0.5: 0.65 / 4.42 / 3.49 %, PASS on all three; at
  certainty 0 (the shipped setting): 0.71 / 4.45 / 4.14 %, so code and IFEval pass and multilingual chat is BETWEEN
  (T106). SSD megabytes per kept token: -46.3 % against -34.9 % for the union rule alone [P, T103].
- **As shipped.** The certainty threshold is 0, so every drafted row counts as sure and every kept expert of a
  drafted row runs at 3 bits; the bonus row stays exact unless it has a draft. The drafter's certainty enters only
  through the cut that decides which rows are drafted at all (section 4.3). The "certainty 0" numbers above are the
  shipped setting's.
- **Status.** Part of the certified configuration on the code sets (section 5): same scores as NAX6-N on HumanEval+ and
  MBPP in both orders [K, F:2026-11-08_0455].
- **Prior art (as recorded).** Per-token mixed precision exists keyed on the router's rank, and per-expert bit widths
  keyed on importance; none found keys precision on the drafter's certainty (NOV, row "3-bit records on drafter-sure
  rows").

### 8.3 Relaxed acceptance restricted to code comments, and its failure when widened

- **The observation.** In code answers, comment text is a small share of positions and a large share of the
  drafter's misses: 13.5 % of positions and 44.7 % of misses on one walk (T1350); on another, comments carry 52 % of
  the first misses in a check [P on K, L350 section 1]. A wrong comment word does not change what the code does.
- **The rule** (EV:PREREG_relax_2026-11-02). On a row whose draft sits inside a '#' comment, the draft is kept if it
  ranks first or second on its row and has at least a quarter of the top token's probability. Everywhere else
  acceptance is exact.
  - Only inside the answer's code fence; never in its first two lines; never in docstrings, strings or code.
  - Never on a comment that is or could become a tool directive (type comments, linter pragmas, an encoding line, a
    shebang).
  - The row must already be inside the comment before its draft, and a draft that starts with a newline stays exact.
  - The span is found by an incremental scan of the answer's text, token by token, so it needs no lookahead.
- **In the engine it is safe so far, and small.** As part of RELAX B it passed decision 4 and the blunder bar on
  code (0 blunders in 25,656 new HumanEval+ tokens), but it adds only 1.8 % to tokens kept per check on HumanEval+
  [K, EV:RESULT_relax_2026-11-05]. A replay had promised x1.07 to x1.16 (T1350). The live confidence cut explains the
  gap: checks that would have continued through a comment are already cut short [P, L350 section 8.2]. In the certified configuration the rule (rank 1-2,
  at least a quarter of the top probability) is on, and it fired 0 times on the 12 HumanEval prompts of the stack's run
  [K, F:2026-11-07_0025].
- **Widened, it fails.** "CD16" keeps the drafter's whole comment and docstring text without checking it, and checks
  only code. On 12 MBPP tasks it kept x1.45 tokens per check and ran +23.7 % faster, and the tasks' tests passed on
  8 of 12 against 11 of 12 without it, in both passes: 3 flips against a line of 2 [K, T855; F:45gb-fast-stack]. The
  answers show comment loops and code that drifts after the drafter's own comments. HumanEval did not show it,
  because its docstrings copy the prompt. CD16 was dropped the same day, with the headline number that contained it
  (section 10.3).
- **The lesson for others:** text that "does not matter" still conditions everything after it. Relaxing the choice
  among the model's own near-top candidates is a different operation from accepting text the model never scored.
- **Tag.** Relaxed and lossy verification is published; the comment-only span was found in neither of two web
  checks (NOV; INV section 1): adapted.

## 9. Comparison with other engines and prior work

Appendix G has our runs of Strata on this machine, Appendix H the published runs on this model, and the prior art is in
Appendix I.

- **Same machine, same prompts [K; Appendix G].** Strata 0.1.58-metal (MIT; github.com/Niko1221/Strata), with its 3-bit
  IQ3_XXS file, experts streamed from the SSD and its MTP layer, decodes 40.6-47.1 tok/s at a lifetime peak of 40.8-41.2
  GB, with its first token in 0.54-1.01 s. Against Strata's arm of the same kind, our lossy decode is x2.5-3.5 its
  decode on code, x1.7 on math and x1.08 on chat; our exact decode is x1.6-2.2 on code and x1.7 on math, and behind on
  chat (37.1-38.3 vs 40.6). Our first token is 2.7-7.0x slower, so Strata wins or ties every whole request of up to 256
  tokens; ours passes it at about 245-260 tokens on code and math, and at about 340 on chat as shipped [P].
- **Published runs [self-reported; Appendix H].** On our machine class, Splash reports 95 tok/s decode and a 1,312 tok/s
  prefill with a 2.4-bit file. On a larger Mac (M5 Max, 128 GB) with every weight resident, Sushi reports 138-169 tok/s
  decode with 2.75-bit weights, and MTPLX 79-126 with 4-bit weights. None of them used our prompts.
- **Prior art [Appendix I].** Every piece of section 7 has published roots: batch invariance (Thinking Machines Lab;
  vLLM and SGLang, on CUDA), drafters and verification, SSD-streamed MoE inference, expert caching and prefetch, mixed
  precision for experts, fewer experts under speculation, and relaxed or lossy verification. To our knowledge no
  published engine for this model combines exact speculative checks, an SSD-streamed rank-tiered store and a certified
  lossy layer; Strata and Sushi combine several of the pieces.

### 10.0b Recent negative results

- **Larger prefill chunks are not exact.** 2,048-8,192-row chunks change the logits (hash equal on 0 of N checks), change

  was not hit and still changes the logits.
- **One MoE call per layer in prefill (PF-B) was not exact at first** (0 of 7-28 logits hashes equal, +15-22 %) [K,
  BM:data/pfb/pfb_report.txt]: the shared expert's one-output gate splits its sums by row count ([KERNELS] section 5.2).
  Kept per chunk, that gate makes PF-B exact (8 / 8, 28 / 28, 22 / 22 logits hashes, x1.12-1.21), but it peaks at
  45.8-46.2 GB, over the line, and does not ship [K, BM:data/pfd/pfd.log part B].
- **Never waiting for cold experts, third form:** +6.2 % whole decode, pooled (141.4 -> 150.2 tok/s), against a +8 %

- **Gaps chain across references.** Each step passed its own gate (NAX6 -3.3 points against REF2-T, the lossy config -3.3

  [K, F:2026-11-07_0550, F:2026-11-08_0455]. Gates against the immediate predecessor do not bound drift over several steps.
- **Lossy runs are not reproducible token for token** (8 of 12 prompts equal between two passes; section 6.1); a lossy
  configuration has to be certified under two prompt orders.


### 10.1 Never waiting for the SSD: passes the quality screen, loses in the engine

- **Idea.** A missing top-2 expert is read in the background and its term is added one layer late, so the GPU never
  waits (the deferral family, here for the 45 GB FAST mode).
- **Quality screen: PASS.** Code flips 0.68 % against 0.74 % for its twin at a 208-slot pool (T50).
- **Engine: KILL of the first form.** -2.8 % (153.5 vs 157.9 tok/s in the same run, on the steady metric then
  registered: section 10.0), although kept tokens rose 4.6 %.
  The check grew from 68.8 to 75.3 ms (T209).
- **Why.** The form did not cut the wait: the gate service went from 14.0 to 15.6 ms per check, and about 6.5 ms of
  late work was added in the per-layer pipeline (T209c; the engine's owner, 6 Nov). Its own calls still waited on the
  loader's release for cold optional experts and cold sidecars (LL, 'NEVER-WAIT v3').
- **Why the price was wrong.** The prediction (+10-15 %) treated 13.5-15 ms of gate service per check as removable
  waiting. It is a spin bound that out-of-order execution already overlaps with compute.
- **Status.** A revised form that drops the cold optional reads is built and bit-checked. It needs its own quality
  arm before a speed test, so the family is not closed.
- **Note.** Both arms ran on a stack that still contained CD16 (section 10.3). The
  comparison is within one run.

### 10.2 Fewer expert groups on sure rows ("split"): the tile price was 2-3x too high

- **Idea.** On rows the drafter is sure of, compute only ranks 0-1 plus experts the check loads anyway.
- **Quality screen.** PASS on code (0.92 % and 1.31 % flips at certainty 0.99 and 0.9, line 1.34 %), KILL on chat
  (T553, T552).
- **Engine.** Computed expert groups fell to x0.63 and x0.49, yet check + commit fell only 6 and 5 %, and kept tokens
  fell 4.2 and 1.7 %: net about 0 to +1.5 % against a priced +11-19 % (T610; F:flat2). On the matrix-unit kernel:
  -1.3 %, with kept tokens -5.7 % (T744).
- **Lesson.** On this GPU the expert time is mostly per-row work, not per-group reads (11.1 ms at 2 rows, 57.0 at 16,
  all resident). Counting tiles, groups or bytes overpredicted the gain two to three times. A lossy cut also lowers
  acceptance, and that loss must be in the price.

### 10.3 Wholesale acceptance of comments (CD16), and a withdrawn 157.9 tok/s

- The stack measured 157.9 tok/s on 12 HumanEval prompts (passes 157.2 / 158.1) and was written up as a headline.
  It included CD16. CD16 then failed quality on MBPP (section 8.3), and the number was withdrawn before publication
  (F:45gb-fast-stack, WITHDRAWN).
- **Lesson.** A lever must be gated on a workload that can expose it. HumanEval could not: its docstrings are copies
  of the prompt, so accepting them unchecked changes nothing. A guarded form (T16) scored BETWEEN on 12 MBPP prompts (one flipped task) and was not
  adopted [K, F:2026-11-07_0025].

### 10.4 The 12 GB FAST mode failed same-scores

- **The mode.** A cheap first pass with only the top-2 experts certifies a token when its top probability is at
  least theta; other rows are recomputed with the full computation.
- **Result.** At theta 0.8 on code and 0.95 on chat: HumanEval+ 80.0 vs 91.7 (0 / 7, p 0.02) and IFEval 84.0 vs 90.0
  FAIL; math passes; memory passes (at most 10.84 GB) (EV:RESULT_t0_2026-11-05). Truncations and loops rose from
  13 / 2 to 23 / 7.
- **What it teaches.**
  - The decision map had predicted it: the same setting flips 1.63 % of code rows, over the 1.34 % line (T54).
  - The certificate itself is token-accurate (0-1 divergences in about 3,400 tokens at every theta). The damage comes
    through the context: the cheap pass also writes the attention and recurrent state that later tokens read (T1265).
    A certificate on the token says nothing about the state it leaves behind.
- **Status.** Since then theta 0.8 and its theta 0.9 fallback failed HumanEval+ in both prompt orders (83.3 and 86.7
  vs 95.0 %; EV:RESULT_t80ck1u_2026-11-07). The code-valid point (theta 0.95, map 1.22 %), modelled at 35.9 tok/s [P, a
  calibrated replay], measured 27.9 tok/s whole decode, pooled, on 12 HumanEval + 12 MBPP [K, F12R:tab12f]. The mode is
  labelled "lossy, failed its gate".

### 10.5 A withdrawn "12 GB exact at 40.8 tok/s"

- A public note claimed 40.8 tok/s, bit-exact, in 11.35 GB. A re-check found that the measured arm was a FAST mode:
  it commits rows certified by the cheap pass without the full check. It matched the verified loop on 5 of 16
  prompts, and the quality screen scores its certificate (top-2 experts, theta 0.8) a code KILL at 1.63 %. The
  verified loop runs 18.3 tok/s whole decode, pooled, on 12 HumanEval + 12 MBPP (whole request 13.1, first token 4.29 s,
  lifetime peak 11.14 GB) [K, F12R:tab12e]. The note now carries a dated correction (F:12gb-exact-40tps; F12).
- The same week showed that the verified loop itself is not bit-identical to one-token decoding [K, F12 'What the 12 GB exact loop (arm ex) is exact to']. The
  word "exact" had been used for three different properties: bit-identical to one-token decoding, verified by the
  full model, and reproducible.
- **Lesson.** An arm's name is not its definition. The exactness check had been run on 4 of the 16 prompts. An audit
  over every prompt, as in section 3.2, would likely have caught it before the note went out [G].

### 10.6 Agent turns, and two lossy parts that compound on chat

- **Agent turns.** Plain FAST wrote 85 low-probability tokens in 19,749 on 17 long agent turns (about 43 per
  10,000); 75 of them are inside the written file content. Short edits are clean; long file writes carry the failure
  (EV:RESULT_agentfast_2026-11-06).
- **Cause.** With QOM-KEEP off, blunders fall from 21 to 4 (forward order) and 18 to 0 (reverse): the cache-dependent
  keep carries them. Other parts do not move the count (EV:RESULT_agentbisect_2026-11-06).
- **Run-to-run dependence is wider than one lever.** With QOM-KEEP off, the two prompt orders still differ within
  the first tokens. The check logs show the drafted row counts differing about 27 tokens before the committed tokens
  do, so the drafts carry history. If any FAST kernel's bits depend on a check's row count, even exact acceptance
  then depends on the drafts (same source, addendum). The exact base must be identical in both orders.
  It is: every exact run equals one-token decoding, in any order (section 3.2).
- **Chat.** Relaxed acceptance alone scores IFEval 87, 8-bit HC mixers alone 89, both together 84 against 90 for the
  reference: a failure neither shows alone (EV:RESULT_relax_drift_2026-11-06, section 3).

### 10.7 A data-aware 2.4-bit trellis store did 1.6-2.5x the damage its error predicted

- Priced from its weight-error ratio against 3-bit truncation, the store measured much worse on the decision map:
  1.45 / 9.54 / 10.44 % flips against lines of 1.34 / 5.42 / 3.69 (F:trellis_mse_miss).
- Two independent derivations agree that these errors cost about 1.7-3.5x more KL divergence per unit of
  expert-output error than truncation errors do. The mechanism is open.
- **Lesson.** For this model, weight or output error cannot rank a data-aware code against plain truncation without
  a divergence screen on real prompts.

### 10.8 A fixed cost that did not fall, and a timing method that was wrong

- With every lossy lever of 6 Nov morning on, the fixed cost per check was still 53.7 ms, against a KILL line of 36 ms
  ([KERNELS] 6.5). 350 tok/s on code at 12 kept
  tokens would need about 25 ms [P, F:flat2].
- An earlier fit had put a dependent dispatch at 12-20 us, which pointed at fewer, fused kernels. The direct
  measurement is 1.6 us ([KERNELS] 6.3); the loss was in narrow kernels. Two rebuilt kernels from that programme (a
  row-parallel GDN step and a replacement HC kernel) were bit-correct and slower than what they replaced (LL,
  'GDN-CHUNK', 'HC-K2C').
- A split that was thought live was inert for a night because another fast path bypassed it (F:flat2, caveat). The
  engaged-path counters of section 3.2 exist for this reason.

### 10.9 From the ledger: ideas an outside reader would likely propose

One line each; numbers and sources in `TR:TRIED_SHORT.md` and the full ledger.

- **Look-ahead expert prefetch:** pays at 24 GB (+9-10 %), KILL at 45 GB (wrong guesses evict).
- **mmap or the page cache instead of direct reads:** KILL (mmap about 5x slower).
- **Cache-aware routing or substituting resident experts:** KILL; not exact, and small. Expert independence
  (Appendix F.6) explains why.
- **Fewer experts per token (top-8):** KILL at the strict gate, with or without renormalisation.
- **All experts at 3 bits; any 2-bit tier:** KILL.
- **Draft trees:** KILL; the best tree is -10 to +2 % against the best chain at equal cost.
- **Wider (32-row) checks:** KILL at today's per-row cost (-1.3 %); they pay only below about 0.9 ms per row (KP).
- **Self-speculation, layer skip, early exit:** KILL; exiting after layer 40 agrees with the full model on 5.7 % of
  code positions.
- **Exact speculative sampling instead of greedy matching:** KILL; acceptance +0.003 to +0.014.
- **Drafter retrains:** on-policy data +0.13 kept per doubling; 2x capacity +0.18; one reseed alone moves code by
  0.16. Only the data slope is above noise (KP).
- **Reusing the previous position's MoE output:** the one fully new idea; KILL on bytes (union only x0.91) (NOV).
- **Matrix units for experts at short checks:** KILL at the 1 Nov operating point (+1.8 %); adopted later, once
  checks were longer and the records were in the kernel's native layout (T675, T527).
- **Compiling the verify graph:** KILL (-0.7 ms; the graph build was not the cost then).
- **Several streams in one pass:** aggregate throughput only; per stream it is slower than solo (F:union-law).

### 10.10 How far ahead can a forecast help the expert cache? (CPU replays; measurements, no method claim)

A forecast made 8 checks ahead of the experts each layer will need could steer the expert cache. We measured its
ceilings on recorded traces: 176 checks of six prompts, replayed on the CPU through the engine's cache at the 45 GB line
(K 266 slots per layer) and the 24 GB line (K 147) [K, FAR:far_ceiling.txt; F:2026-11-04_2210_far-ahead-foresight]. No
forecaster was built into the engine, and the GPU test that would score a real far draft did not run.
- **The kept path is not enough.** A perfect forecast of only the rows the checks keep cuts misses by 20.3 % against LRU
  at K 266 (26.3 % at K 147), at recall 0.68 (0.76): the rows the checks reject hold the other 32 % of the reuse [K,
  FAR:far_ceiling.txt].
- **Misses are not stalls.** Perfect foresight of every row of the next 8 checks cuts misses by 33.7 % but the layers
  that wait on the SSD by only 5.4 % (Belady's optimum: 55.3 % and 19.3 %) [K, same file].
- **Tokens do not predict deep-layer routes; states do better.** From the token alone, per-row recall@10 of a row's
  experts is 0.24-0.28 in layers 8-47 (a table built from the other prompts; about 0.40 from the same token's previous
  occurrence), and the best honest token map cuts misses by 6.0 % [K, same file]. A ridge map from the 125B's own
  layer-1 MoE inputs of the future rows, standing in for a far drafter's states, reaches recall 0.70 / precision 0.64
  and 14.6 % fewer misses at K 266 (stalled layers -1.4 %), and 11.9 % at K 147 [K, FAR:far_ridge.txt]. Both are the best
  of 16 settings, each fitted leave-one-prompt-out but picked on the same replay; the 16 span 7.9-14.6 % and 6.2-11.9 %.
- **Prior art.** Draft-fed expert forecasts in the literature look ahead one round (SP-MoE, 2510.10302) or at most 32
  tokens (Specter, 2511.14102). The state-to-route map itself is adapted from SP-MoE and DraftExpert (2607.24434).

To our knowledge, none of these findings has been published before. For each we give what we found, how we found it,
the evidence, why it happens (what is measured [K] and what is still a hypothesis [G]), what it gives, how it could
be improved, and where else it could apply. One of the four is an idea that did not pay off (12.3).

### 12.1 The SSD's wake penalty at sub-millisecond idle gaps

**What we found.** On this MacBook Pro's internal SSD, the first read after an idle gap of 0.25 ms or more is slower. A streamed
MoE runs into this on every layer, because its expert reads come in one burst per layer with GPU work in between. Reads
spaced closely enough keep the drive awake and remove the penalty.

**How we found it.** We timed single reads after controlled idle gaps in micro-benchmarks that load no model, and split
the engine's loader cost per call into its parts (section 8.1).

**Evidence.** After 0.10 ms of idle there is no penalty, and from 0.25 ms on the full one [K, MS:data/ssdmicro.txt]. It
costs +50-65 us per burst after 0.25-1 ms gaps and +100-120 us after 20 ms, and a CPU core kept busy changes nothing
[K, T93]. A trickle of 4 KB reads at a real spacing of at most 270 us removes the whole +44 us; 400 us or more leaves
half [K, T44]. In the engine, a 50 us keepalive saves about 3 ms per check [K, BM:docs/NIGHT_1104.md:37]; it is part of
the exact default's +12.1 % bundle, and no A/B isolates it on the final engine.

**Why it happens.** Measured: the penalty depends on the idle gap and not on CPU load, and reads that end 0.25 ms or
more before the next demand read let the drive doze again [K, MS:data/ssdmicro3.txt
