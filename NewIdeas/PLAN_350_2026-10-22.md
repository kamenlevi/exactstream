# The road to ~350 tok/s for one chat (NewIdeas -> Kamen, Thu 10-22, 07:48)
Details: ~/NewIdeas/GAPS.md section "C350"; Chat1000's model ~/Chat1000/REPORT.md section 11 (scripts/path350.py).

## What we measure
Visible tokens per second while one chat decodes (one user, greedy, prompt reading excluded) on your M5 Pro 64 GB, with the
model in RAM (3-bit pools for all experts, ~60 GB, the Mac otherwise idle). Exact = identical to the v3 reference, preferred;
non-exact routes are tested and reported separately, and adopting one is your call. Two workloads, always reported
separately: general text (code / prose / math prompts) and your real agentic turns.

## Where we are and the arithmetic
visible tok/s = (1 / (1 - F)) x A / T_pass
F = share of tokens that need no pass (copies); A = tokens accepted per verify pass; T_pass = time per pass.
- Today: ~56 tok/s on your agentic turns (corrected from ~65 at 08:37; blocks simulated along each turn), ~45-53 on prose
  (stock engine, the 125B's own drafter, measured acceptance).
- A pass of 4 rows takes 37 ms with every expert in RAM, already near the hardware's limit, so faster kernels alone can't get
  there. The drafter's guesses are right 85 / 71 / 61 / 51 % of the time for the 1st / 2nd / 3rd / 4th token.
- With today's drafter, every other lever stacked gives at most ~110 tok/s (~145 on agentic turns with copy pointers). So
  350 needs ~2.4-2.9x from one thing: a much better drafter.

## The levers, tagged (published / published but untested here / new)
| lever | gain | tag |
|---|---|---|

All together: ~313 tok/s on agentic turns (~416 with copy pointers), ~402 on prose. Without the drafter: <= ~130.

## The new bet in flight
The drafter decides 350, and no published drafter reaches what we need. So the first, cheap step (today, ~15 min of GPU)
measures the ceiling: how often does a near-copy of the 125B itself (all its dense weights, 10 % of its experts) agree with the
full model, token by token, on your agentic turns and on general text? If even a near-copy stays below ~92 %, no small drafter
will get there, and 350 is out of reach on agentic turns with this model (code and math may still make it). If it is ~95 % or
more, we start the drafter program: a block drafter built from the 125B's own drafter layer, trained on the 125B's own outputs
(several local GPU nights). This replaces last night's "not now" recommendation, which was about the old one-token-at-a-time
drafter.

## The drafter program, concretely (08:33)
We don't have to train a drafter from zero. A published drafter (DFlash, open license) for the 35B model is already on the
Mac, and the 125B uses exactly the same vocabulary. Only a small 26M-parameter adapter has to be learned so it can read the
125B's internal state. First a 30-minute test without any training (today). If it beats the current drafter, the full program
takes about two weeks with ~4 GPU nights, with a kill test after week 1. Expected: ~1.5-2x more tokens per pass on code than
today's drafter, which keeps 350 on code within reach; on your agentic turns it likely falls short of 350.

## The genuinely new bet, named (08:44)
How we get training data for the drafter without spending GPU days generating it: we take your real session text and, for
each spot, count only the stretch up to where the text first differs from what the 125B itself would write. That stretch is
exactly the 125B's own output, at no generation cost. A prior-art check (Chat1000) found nearby published ideas (Spec-AUF,
GRIFFIN, CTC-drafter, DistillSpec) but not this cut, so we tag it NEW. Everything else on the 350 road is published work,
untested on this model and hardware.

## Trained routing, reality check (09:16, TrainLab)
The plan counted 1.7x from training the routing so neighbouring tokens reuse experts. No published after-the-fact method
gets that far; ~1.3-1.5x is realistic, and it changes the model's routing (non-exact: your call to adopt). A two-hour test on
the 35B decides whether we can train it cheaply, layer by layer. With the realistic number, 350 on code needs a drafter a
little better than ~9 tokens per block.

## A training-free alternative (09:44, TrainLab)
Instead of training the routing, a verify pass can choose its experts jointly: the ~32 experts that matter most across all 16
rows, with each row using its best experts within that set. Published as 'batch-aware routing' (XShare 2026; OEA, -39 % decode
latency on a 30B MoE). On the 125B's routing traces this needs ~2x fewer experts per pass, with an estimated quality cost
inside the 12 GB setup's accepted band, and no training at all. It is non-exact (your call). TrainLab's 35B run measures it
for real next to the trained-routing variants.

## Trained routing: dropped; the training-free version stays (11:03)
Training the routing cheaply (layer by layer) made things worse than simply capping experts. Dropped. The training-free

quality cost of caps. Its quality on the 125B is measured next (30 minutes). It changes outputs slightly, so adopting it is your

## Shared expert sets measured on hardware (16:48, TrainLab turn14)
On the 24 GB main track (experts streamed from the SSD, 4-token checks, MTP drafter), sharing a 16-expert set per check ran
+32 % on code (34.4 -> 45.4 tok/s) and +22 % on a 6K-token prompt; the time per check fell 14-21 % and SSD misses per check to
0.56-0.68x (the trace replay had predicted 0.36-0.46x: about 1.5x optimistic). Quality is over the strict limit on math and
instructions (1.6-1.8x), so it is not yet a passing option. This is the 4-token case at 24 GB. The 350 table above uses the
16-token in-RAM case (31 ms per check with a 32-expert set), whose quality (R=16 KLD) is still unmeasured; given the replay's
optimism here, read that row's 31 ms as a best case until it is measured.
